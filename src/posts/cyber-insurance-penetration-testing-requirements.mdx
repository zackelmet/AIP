Cyber insurers have become much more specific about security controls. Applications that once asked a handful of yes/no questions now request evidence: multi-factor authentication coverage, backup testing, endpoint detection, and — increasingly — **penetration testing**.

A pentest is not a magic discount coupon. But a current, validated assessment can answer underwriter questions directly, demonstrate that you test your environment, and support better terms. Here is what insurers typically want, what does not satisfy the requirement, and how to time testing around renewal.

## Why insurers ask for penetration testing

Underwriters are trying to estimate the probability and cost of a breach. A pentest report gives them evidence about how you find and fix exploitable weaknesses. It also shows that security testing is a repeatable process rather than a one-time cleanup.

Insurers are not usually evaluating whether your report is “good” in an academic sense. They are looking for signals: Do you test externally? Do you validate findings? Do you remediate? Can you show progress over time?

## What underwriters typically want

Requirements vary by insurer, policy size, and industry. Common requests include:

| What they ask for | What satisfies it |
|---|---|
| External penetration test | A scoped assessment of internet-facing systems, not just an automated scan |
| Validated findings | Confirmed, exploitable issues with proof of concept — not raw scanner output |
| Remediation evidence | Evidence that critical and high findings were fixed or accepted with compensating controls |
| Testing frequency | At least annual testing, and more often for high-change environments |
| Report or attestation | A client-ready report or a short attestation letter summarizing scope, dates, and results |
| Retest confirmation | Verification that remediation actually closed the finding |

Some insurers also ask about internal testing, social engineering, or cloud configuration review. Match the scope to the questions you are actually being asked.

## What does not satisfy the requirement

A few common substitutes fall short:

- **A raw vulnerability scan export.** Scans are useful, but they do not prove exploitability and often contain false positives.
- **A report with no remediation evidence.** Underwriters want to see the follow-through, not just a list of issues.
- **A stale report.** A two-year-old pentest against an environment that has changed significantly is weak evidence.
- **An unanswered questionnaire.** If you claim testing but cannot produce a report or attestation, the answer is difficult to defend.

## Timing your test around renewal

The best time to test is **before** the insurer asks, with enough time to remediate critical findings and obtain a retest if needed.

A practical sequence:

1. **Start 8–12 weeks before renewal.** This leaves time for scoping, testing, remediation, and a retest.
2. **Scope to the insurer's questions.** If they ask about external exposure, test external assets. If they ask about a specific application, include it.
3. **Fix critical and high findings first.** Underwriters care most about the issues that could cause a claim.
4. **Request an attestation letter.** A short summary of scope, dates, methodology, and results is often more useful to an underwriter than a 100-page technical report.
5. **Retest after remediation.** A clean retest is strong evidence that your process works.

## How to share results without oversharing

You do not need to send a full technical report to every stakeholder. A redacted report or attestation letter can answer the underwriter's questions while protecting sensitive details about internal systems and exploit techniques.

If the insurer wants more detail, provide it under NDA or through a secure portal. Keep the full report available for your own team and for auditors who need it.

## What a good insurance-ready report includes

- Scope and testing dates.
- Methodology and standards followed (for example, OWASP Top 10, NIST SP 800-115, PTES).
- Confirmed findings with severity and business impact.
- Remediation guidance.
- Evidence of retesting or a remediation summary.
- A clear statement of limitations and exclusions.

If your current provider does not offer an attestation or retest, ask before renewal. It is much easier to plan than to scramble.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a penetration test guarantee lower insurance premiums?**
No. Underwriters consider many factors. A pentest can support your application and demonstrate controls, but it does not guarantee pricing or coverage.

**Will a vulnerability scan satisfy a cyber insurance requirement?**
Usually not on its own. Insurers typically want validated testing. A scan can supplement a pentest but rarely replaces it.

**How old can a penetration test be?**
Most insurers expect testing within the last 12 months. If your environment has changed significantly, a newer test is stronger evidence.

**Do I need internal testing too?**
Only if the insurer asks or if internal risk is material. Many applications focus on external exposure first.
